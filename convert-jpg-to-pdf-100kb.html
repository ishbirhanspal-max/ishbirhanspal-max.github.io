Why Government and Education Portals Have Strict PDF Size Limits

To truly solve the PDF size problem, it helps to understand why these limits exist in the first place. Government web portals, university admissions systems, and banking KYC platforms were largely designed between 2005 and 2015, when average internet connection speeds in India and many emerging markets ranged from 512 Kbps to 2 Mbps. At those speeds, processing a 5MB PDF upload could take over 30 seconds and consume significant server bandwidth when multiplied across thousands of simultaneous users during peak admission seasons.

Even as broadband infrastructure has improved dramatically, the upload limits in government portal software have not been updated because changing these limits requires comprehensive testing, procurement approval cycles, and updates to legacy government IT systems. This is why UPSC, SSC, state PSC portals, and many university admission systems continue to enforce 100KB and 200KB limits that were set over a decade ago.

Understanding PDF File Size: What Takes Up Space?

Many users are surprised to discover that a scanned photo-based PDF is enormous while a similar document saved from Microsoft Word is tiny. The reason lies in what each PDF actually contains:

Image-Based PDFs vs Vector-Text PDFs

When you photograph a document and convert it to PDF, the entire page is stored as a rasterized bitmap image. A camera photo at 3024x4032 pixels (12 megapixels) at standard JPEG quality contains roughly 3-5MB of image data. The PDF wrapper around this image adds minimal overhead, so the resulting PDF is essentially as large as the original photo.

By contrast, a PDF exported from Microsoft Word or Google Docs contains vector-based text and scalable graphics. Each character is stored as a mathematical description of its outline shape rather than millions of coloured pixels. A 10-page text document in Word might generate a 150KB PDF because text is extremely compressible in vector form.

Colour vs Grayscale: The Single Biggest Win

For document scans containing only black text on a white page, converting to grayscale before PDF conversion is the single most effective technique. A colour JPEG stores three bytes per pixel (Red, Green, Blue). A grayscale JPEG stores only one byte per pixel — an immediate 66% reduction in data before any compression is applied. For a typical marks certificate or government-issued document scanned as colour, switching to grayscale alone can reduce the output PDF from 400KB to under 120KB without any visible reduction in text clarity.

DPI and Resolution Calibration

Resolution, measured in Dots Per Inch (DPI), describes how many pixels are packed into one inch of the scanned image. For human reading purposes, 150 DPI is sufficient for clear text in physical documents scanned for digital submission. Most phone cameras scanning a document at 30cm distance capture the equivalent of 300-400 effective DPI, which is far higher than needed for legibility but significantly inflates file size. Downscaling from 300 DPI to 150 DPI reduces the total pixel count by 75%, which translates to proportional file size savings.

Our Image Compressor allows you to specify a target width in pixels, allowing you to calibrate the effective DPI before conversion. For A4 documents at 150 DPI, a target width of 1240 pixels is appropriate. For ID cards and certificates with smaller print, 1600-1800 pixels at reduced JPEG quality (70-80%) typically achieves 40-80KB output files with excellent clarity.
